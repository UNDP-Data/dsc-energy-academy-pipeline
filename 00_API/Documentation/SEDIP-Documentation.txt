Sustainable Energy Digital Intelligence Platform - Documentation
Updated 30 August 2025
The Sustainable Energy Digital Intelligence Platform is UNDP’s flagship digital solution for just, inclusive, and data-driven energy transitions. Developed over two years, the platform represents a digital public good that translates UNDP’s policy expertise, global knowledge, and country experience into actionable intelligence.The platform supports countries in integrating sustainable energy into development pathways, advancing UNDP’s Strategic Plan 2022–2025, and positioning UNDP as a global leader in digital solutions for sustainable energy. The platform features five innovative pillars, each addressing a critical dimension of sustainable energy for development:I. Sustainable Energy Academy — 80+ hours of learning across 1,000 pages of expert-authored content, available in seven languages, including 70+ country case studies and 300+ original charts and diagrams. Developed with regional/thematic specialists, seven academic partners, and 100+ expert UN Volunteers, the Academy represents the next era of training and knowledge exchange.II. Energy AI and Knowledge Graph — a standalone and Academy-integrated tool that surfaces insights and visualizations from a curated database of publications, policies, and plans.III. Energy Geospatial Platform — customizable geospatial maps of energy access, equity, and investment opportunities. Features UNDP-produced data products developed with IBM and partner institutions, such as the Clean Energy Equity Index and High Resolution Energy Access models.IV. Multidimensional Energy Indices — an interactive tool for exploring indices that analyze pathways to equity in energy transitions. Includes UNDP-developed indices (e.g., Cooling Preparedness Index and CEEI-Indicator) alongside global indices such as MEPI, RISE, and the WEC Trilemma.V. Energy Moonshot Tracker (UNDP users only) — a portfolio database covering nearly 400 sustainable energy projects active during the Strategic Plan 2022–2025. It applies a unique methodology for tracking direct and indirect beneficiaries and provides a standardized mapping of UNDP’s impact beyond vertical funds.The Digital Intelligence Platform is an integrated offer developed through consultations across UNDP teams. By combining learning, AI, geospatial data, indices, and portfolio intelligence, it directly advances the ambitions of the UNDP Strategic Plan 2022–2025.I. Sustainable Energy Academy — Technical DocumentationA. Purpose, Scope, and UsersThe Sustainable Energy Academy is the learning foundation of the Sustainable Energy Digital Intelligence Platform. Its purpose is to equip UNDP country offices, government counterparts, development partners, civil society, academia, and private sector actors with the knowledge, methods, and tools to design, fund, and implement just, inclusive, and data-driven energy transitions.The Academy is embedded in UNDP’s practitioner ecosystem and links learning directly to country programming and portfolio design. It connects learners to the Sustainable Energy Community of Practice (more than 2,400 practitioners), ensuring that training is continuously informed by field needs and peer exchange.The Academy was developed with seven academic partners: Federal University of Rio de Janeiro, Strathmore University, Khalifa University, Peking University, Jawaharlal Nehru University, Central European University, and Columbia University.B. Content and Curriculum ArchitectureB.1 Curriculum Overview and Learning OutcomesThe Academy delivers a structured program totaling more than 80 hours across a comprehensive, chaptered curriculum. Graduates are expected to:* Diagnose access gaps.* Design equity-centered policies and projects.* Structure and de-risk finance.* Integrate mitigation and adaptation in national strategies.* Apply geospatial, index, and AI tools to operationalize evidence-based targeting.B.2 Modules and CoverageThe curriculum is organized into eight modules, each with clearly defined scope, objectives, and applied exercises:Module 1. Introduction to Sustainable Energy for Development — energy systems as a driver for development; SDG7 context; energy–prosperity–environment dilemmas and interlinkages with other SDGs.Module 2. Energy Access and Inclusive Energy Services — access as a human right; fuel/energy poverty; current gaps and barriers; intersectional and community-led approaches; financing and policy frameworks.Module 3. Just Energy Transition — principles and evolution; Global South challenges (fossil dependence, land and infrastructure constraints, access gaps); worker transition and social protection; partnerships including JET-P.Module 4. Sustainable Energy Finance — fundamentals and trends; public finance and subsidy reform; blended finance; instruments such as thematic bonds, debt swaps, carbon finance, and emerging digital/AI solutions.Module 5. Energy Governance for a Just Energy Transition — inclusive, accountable, effective institutions; multi-level governance; civic engagement and oversight; policy coherence, foresight, and de-risking.Module 6. The Climate Change–Energy Nexus — energy emissions and climate impacts; mitigation via renewables; adaptation of energy systems; decarbonization of end-use sectors with equity and resilience.Module 7. Data in Sustainable Energy — the energy data value chain; transparency and equity in decision-making; integration with geospatial and AI tools.Module 8. Renewable Energy Technologies and Innovations — enabling environments for innovation (carbon pricing, ETS, incentives); decentralized resources; EV integration; storage; circular economy approaches.B.3 Chapter and Lesson Structure (Examples)Each module comprises chapters and lessons with explicit durations, objectives, and assets. For example:* Module 4 includes a cross-sectional finance chapter that foregrounds partnerships, gender integration, and regional priorities for equitable, effective finance.* Module 8 contains a chapter on policy and market mechanisms for innovation, and a lesson on how decentralized resources and EV integration are reshaping future systems.* An “Action” chapter consolidates practical steps and partnership ecosystems, closing with a call to action.B.4 Content Data Model and MetadataAcademy content is represented in structured JSON to ensure consistency and automation across the platform. Typical objects contain:* id (module.chapter[.lesson]) and title* icon (SVG), header image (URL + caption), and optional frame (URL)* description and aiPrompt (concise guidance for AI-assisted tasks)* estimatedTime and progress* lessons[] (array of lesson objects with media, prompts, and status)* optional tooltip summary for chapter-level toolkits and overviewsB.5 Media and AssetsLessons reference over 300 original charts and diagrams and use image/frame assets to support interactive learning. Media are linked by URL with captions and optimized for web and low-bandwidth delivery.B.6 Languages and LocalizationCore content is available in seven languages. Terminology aligns with UN usage and sector standards to ensure accurate translation of technical terms across modules and assessments.C. Delivery, Integration, and User ExperienceC.1 Pedagogy and InteractionThe Academy emphasizes case-based, applied learning grounded in UNDP and partner practice. More than 70 country case studies and embedded knowledge checks are included throughout. Modules culminate in scenario-based assessments such as drafting policy notes, investment memos, or portfolio briefs, requiring learners to apply tools (geospatial layers, indices, AI) to realistic tasks.C.2 Integration with the Platform PillarsThe Academy is integrated with the other four pillars of the Digital Intelligence Platform:* Energy AI and Knowledge Graph — lessons embed “ask the AI” tasks to retrieve curated evidence from a knowledge base with citations.* Geospatial Platform — exercises use electrification, resource potential, socioeconomic, and equity layers for diagnostics and pipeline origination.* Multidimensional Energy Indices — learners experiment with the CEEI and other indices on Geohub, customizing weights and running simulations.* Energy Moonshot Tracker — learners interpret portfolio patterns and practice standardized beneficiary conversion methods validated by country offices.C.3 Access Model and AudiencesThe Academy serves both UNDP internal staff and external partners. It is optimized for broad access, with low-bandwidth alternatives, screen-reader compatibility, and alt text for all figures.D. Assessment, Certification, and CredentialsD.1 Assessment Model* Chapter-level checks for understanding.* Module-level scenario assessments using Platform tools.* Rubrics emphasize technical accuracy, use of evidence, and equity integration.D.2 Certification FrameworkThe Academy offers tiered credentials:* Foundational Certification — completion of entry modules (Access, Governance, Finance).* Advanced Certification — completion of all modules plus capstone assessments.* Specialized Badges — thematic mastery (Finance, Data/GIS, Just Transition).D.3 Capstone WorkflowsCapstones simulate real programming tasks, such as:* Drafting an investment concept for decentralized renewables with a gender-responsive finance structure.* Designing a subnational equity-targeting strategy using index weights and local data layers.E. Operations: Governance, QA, Data Protection, Analytics, and RoadmapE.1 Governance and Quality AssuranceContent governance combines thematic leads and regional reviewers with an editorial board. Contributions come from seven academic partners and more than 100 expert UN Volunteers. Peer review, sensitivity review, and gender/inclusion checks ensure quality and relevance.E.2 Partnerships and ContributorsAcademic partners include: Federal University of Rio de Janeiro, Strathmore University, Khalifa University, Peking University, Jawaharlal Nehru University, Central European University, and Columbia University.E.3 Data Protection and EthicsAI-supported tasks require citations. Sensitive topics are routed through validation and human-in-the-loop review. Data use conforms to UNDP policies on privacy and responsible AI.E.4 Learning Analytics and PerformancePerformance metrics include:* Enrollment and completion rates.* Assessment scores and time-to-proficiency.* Evidence of applied learning (e.g., investment briefs, policy proposals).Insights feed into the roadmap and inform the Energy Moonshot Tracker.II. Energy AI and Knowledge Graph — Technical DocumentationA. Purpose and ScopeThe Energy AI and Knowledge Graph pillar combines advances in natural language processing with a curated, structured knowledge base to provide fast, transparent, and reliable access to sustainable energy information. It is designed as both a standalone public interface and an embedded tool within the Academy, allowing learners and practitioners to ask questions, retrieve policy-relevant insights, and visualize connections across the energy system.The primary objectives are to:* Enable rapid, evidence-based policy analysis.* Surface relationships between policies, actors, technologies, and development outcomes.* Provide interactive visualization of energy data, reducing fragmentation and improving decision-making.B. System ArchitectureB.1 Data integration layerThe system connects multiple categories of content, including:* National energy policies and plans.* International and regional publications.* UNDP project outputs and reports.* Global indicator datasets (e.g., World Bank, IEA, IRENA).* Selected real-time APIs.At launch, the knowledge base includes a core corpus of curated publications, policies, and plans. Expansion to include thousands of national documents and comprehensive datasets is planned for future releases.B.2 Query processingUser queries pass through a modular processing pipeline:1. Entity recognition and query parsing — identifying countries, technologies, sectors, or actors.2. Semantic retrieval — using embeddings and similarity search to locate relevant documents and data.3. Dataset mapping — linking queries to structured indicators or geospatial layers where available.4. Action triggers — activating analytics or chart generation when a quantitative response is required.B.3 Response synthesisResults are compiled and presented in natural language, with:* Explicit citations back to source documents.* Linked entities displayed in the Knowledge Graph view.* Optional charts or tables when data outputs are available.B.4 Validation layerTo ensure reliability, outputs undergo:* Citation checks (preventing hallucinated references).* Sensitivity and data quality checks for fragile contexts.* Rule-based filters for incomplete or contradictory results.C. Interfaces and User ExperienceC.1 Standalone interface* A conversational interface for querying the knowledge base.* Knowledge Graph visualizer showing nodes (e.g., policies, institutions, technologies) and their relationships.* Document viewer for accessing underlying sources.* Query suggestion engine to guide users toward supported query types.C.2 Academy integration* Lessons embed “Ask the AI” tasks.* Learners can pull in real policy documents, case studies, or data directly within module exercises.* AI outputs are citation-rich, allowing learners to practice evidence-based reasoning.C.3 Accessibility* The interface is optimized for public use.* Outputs are available in plain text with citation links.* Visualizations are designed to be interactive but also exportable for low-bandwidth contexts.D. Application and Use CasesThe Energy AI and Knowledge Graph supports a wide range of applications:* Drafting evidence-based policy briefs by retrieving comparable international experiences.* Identifying relationships between subsidy reforms, renewable deployment, and social outcomes.* Exploring how specific technologies (e.g., solar mini-grids, e-mobility) are represented in policy documents.* Linking learners in the Academy directly to relevant country policies and case studies.Future expansion will include integration with geospatial datasets and multidimensional indices, enabling queries such as “Which subnational regions have high renewable potential but low electricity access?” or “Where do equity gaps align with investment flows?”E. RoadmapPlanned enhancements include:* Scaling up the indexed corpus to include thousands of national policies, plans, and strategies.* Expanding linkages to other Platform pillars (Geospatial Platform, Multidimensional Indices, Moonshot Tracker).* Adding advanced visualization capabilities, including timelines of policy evolution and cross-country comparisons.* Incorporating continuous data ingestion pipelines to keep the knowledge base current.III. Energy Geospatial Platform — Technical DocumentationA. Purpose and ScopeThe Energy Geospatial Platform provides a centralized environment for visualizing and analyzing the spatial dimensions of energy access, equity, and investment opportunities. It enables users to customize maps, layer multiple datasets, and generate diagnostics that link energy with development outcomes.The platform is intended for practitioners designing country strategies, governments assessing gaps in access and equity, and investors exploring priority regions for renewable deployment. It translates UNDP’s geospatial research into a tool that is usable in both technical and policy contexts.B. Data FoundationsB.1 Core datasets at launchThe platform aggregates data products developed by UNDP and partners, including:* Electricity access rates and gaps at national and subnational levels.* Renewable energy potential (solar, wind, hydro, biomass).* Infrastructure datasets (transmission, distribution, transport).* Socioeconomic layers (population, poverty, livelihoods).* Equity-focused datasets produced with IBM and academic institutions, such as the Clean Energy Equity Index (CEEI) and high-resolution electricity access models.B.2 Partnerships and provenanceDatasets have been co-developed with IBM, the World Bank, and the University of Michigan, among others. Each dataset carries metadata on source, resolution, year, and licensing. This ensures transparency and supports reproducibility in country diagnostics.B.3 Data model and integration* All layers conform to a standard geospatial schema (GeoJSON/GeoTIFF).* Layers are hosted on UNDP’s Geohub infrastructure for interoperability.* Metadata fields include: dataset name, description, year, source, coverage, resolution, and license.C. Features and User InteractionC.1 Map customizationUsers can:* Toggle layers on/off (e.g., overlay renewable potential on poverty maps).* Adjust transparency and symbology to highlight overlaps.* Zoom to subnational units or regions of interest.C.2 Analytical functions* Measure energy access gaps by region.* Identify renewable resource potential hotspots.* Combine socioeconomic and infrastructure data to highlight underserved but high-potential territories.* Export maps and underlying data for use in reports or planning.C.3 Interoperability* The Geospatial Platform connects with the Academy through embedded exercises in modules on access, finance, and governance.* It is also designed to interoperate with the Multidimensional Indices, where index results can be visualized spatially.* Future integration with the AI + Knowledge Graph will allow natural language queries that call up geospatial visualizations.D. Application and Use CasesThe Geospatial Platform has multiple applications across programming, policy, and investment:* Country diagnostics: visualize electrification gaps, renewable resources, and poverty overlays to prioritize interventions.* Pipeline origination: identify target regions for decentralized renewable energy projects or grid expansion.* Equity analysis: combine the Clean Energy Equity Index with socio-demographic layers to support just transition strategies.* Policy dialogue: use maps as evidence in stakeholder consultations, fostering consensus around priority areas.E. RoadmapPlanned developments include:* Expansion of the dataset library with country-validated national statistics.* Enhanced export features, including automated generation of country briefs.* Development of interactive dashboards linking maps with time-series data.* Integration with Academy capstones, where learners will generate country diagnostics using live geospatial layers.* API development for interoperability with other UNDP platforms and external partner tools.IV. Multidimensional Energy Indices — Technical DocumentationA. Purpose and ScopeThe Multidimensional Energy Indices pillar provides a suite of interactive indices to analyze sustainable energy transitions through an equity lens. Unlike single metrics, these indices combine social, economic, technical, and environmental dimensions to support standardized comparisons, highlight transition priorities, and guide equitable investment.The indices are designed to help:* Governments and policymakers prioritize regions for just transition.* Investors and IFIs identify where concessional or blended finance is most needed.* UNDP country offices align programming with territorial equity gaps.* Civil society and researchers benchmark performance and engage in evidence-based dialogue.B. Index Catalogue at LaunchB.1 UNDP custom indices* Cooling Preparedness Index (CPI)o Focus: readiness of countries and regions to address growing cooling needs linked to climate change, health, and productivity.o Dimensions (illustrative, based on design discussions):* Climate exposure and demand (temperature projections, urban heat).* Access and affordability of cooling solutions.* Socioeconomic vulnerability (health, income, livelihoods).* Enabling environment (policies, technology, infrastructure).o Outputs: preparedness scores at national (and in future, subnational) scale, mapped to highlight populations at risk.* Clean Electricity Equity Index — Indicator View (CEEI-Indicator)o Focus: presents the underlying indicators from the CEEI framework without combining them into a composite score.o Dimensions: urgency, potential, and means (each represented by indicators on social, financial, and technical equity).o Use: allows users to see where deficits are greatest, where renewable potential is strongest, and where enabling capacity is weakest, while retaining full transparency on the data.B.2 Global and reference indices (entire proposed set)* IEA Energy Development Index (EDI) — measures progress in energy development based on household access and per-capita consumption.* Multidimensional Energy Poverty Index (MEPI) — tracks energy poverty through access, affordability, and quality of service.* Sustainable Energy Development Index (SEDI) — combines environmental, economic, and social sustainability of energy systems.* World Bank Regulatory Indicators for Sustainable Energy (RISE) — assesses policy and regulatory frameworks across efficiency, access, and renewables.* World Energy Council Energy Trilemma Index — evaluates countries on security, equity, and environmental sustainability.* Global Energy Institute Energy Security Index — benchmarks vulnerability and resilience of national energy systems.* Global Energy Vulnerability Index — identifies countries most exposed to shocks in supply, price, or climate risks.C. Methodology and Data FoundationsC.1 Standard practices* Outlier treatment, missing-data imputation, normalization, weighting, and aggregation follow documented protocols for composite indices.* Data sources include international statistics (World Bank, IEA, IRENA, WHO), UNDP portfolio data, and custom geospatial datasets (IBM partnership).C.2 Hosting and interoperability* All indices are hosted on UNDP’s Geohub as open geospatial applications.* Metadata (indicators, sources, years, coverage) is published alongside each index for transparency.* The indices are interoperable with the Academy (embedded exercises), and planned for future integration with the AI and Knowledge Graph.D. Features and User Interaction* Visualization — map indices at national and subnational levels; switch between composite scores and indicator dimensions.* Customization — adjust weights for composites where appropriate (future); view alternative normalizations.* Transparency — toggle between composite views and underlying indicators (particularly in the CEEI-Indicator).* Export — download maps, tables, and policy briefs.* Learning mode — Academy exercises use indices for applied tasks (e.g., design a just transition strategy using urgency/potential/means).E. Applications and Use Cases* Policy prioritization: highlight regions most urgent for investment in equitable transitions.* Investment targeting: identify vulnerable regions with strong renewable potential but low capacity, guiding concessional finance.* Program design: integrate multidimensional indices into CPDs and project diagnostics.* Public dialogue: provide a credible, comparable evidence base for building consensus.F. Roadmap* Composite CEEI release: expand from indicator-only to full composite with adjustable weights.* Cooling Preparedness Index expansion: add subnational coverage, time series, and linkages to health and urban resilience data.* New UNDP indices: develop clean cooking equity index and energy–climate resilience indices.* Advanced analytics: add time-series evolution, scenario simulations, and cross-index comparisons.* Integration: connect with the AI + Knowledge Graph to enable queries such as “Which provinces have the highest vulnerability but strongest solar potential?”
UNDP Sustainable Energy Hub
A. Purpose and VisionThe Sustainable Energy Hub (SEH) is UNDP’s integrated platform for advancing sustainable energy for development. It was established to address the complexity and scale of energy transitions by linking policy, technology, finance, and partnerships into a coherent offer.SEH embodies UNDP’s Strategic Plan 2022–2025, which placed sustainable energy at the center of UNDP’s mission to expand choices for a fairer, sustainable future. Its vision is to support countries in realizing just energy transitions—ensuring benefits are widely distributed and negative impacts are minimized for the poorest and most vulnerable communities.B. Global Context and Need* As of 2025, 666 million people lack electricity access and 2.1 billion lack access to clean cooking. Without accelerated action, 645 million people may still be without electricity and 1.8 billion without clean cooking by 2030.* The energy sector is the largest source of greenhouse gas emissions, with fossil fuels accounting for over 81% of global supply. Emissions are highly correlated with development trajectories, especially in UNDP programme countries.* Clean energy investment is concentrated: 85% flows to developed economies, while Africa accounts for only 2%. Developing countries face a shortfall of $2.2 trillion annually for the transition.These gaps demonstrate the urgency of accelerating equitable access and mobilizing finance for developing economies.C. Mandate and Integrated ApproachSEH brings together UNDP’s global, regional, and country-level work on sustainable energy into a single integrated offer. Its comparative advantage lies in:* In-country presence — UNDP’s network in 118 countries, with over 380 interventions, 45% of which target energy access.* Systems perspective — linking energy with health, education, inequality, economy, agriculture, and cities.* Independent technical advice — grounded in deep contextual knowledge.* Partnership-first operating model — working across the UN, with financial institutions, private sector, academia, and civil society.D. Strategic Pillars of Energy for DevelopmentSEH’s approach is anchored in three global pillars:1. Closing the energy access gap — enabling marginalized people and communities to gain access to sustainable, clean energy.2. Accelerating the energy transition — supporting systemic change and efficiency gains through partnerships across governments, private sector, finance, and civil society.3. Catalyzing energy finance — unlocking public and private capital by derisking and reforming financial ecosystems.E.
